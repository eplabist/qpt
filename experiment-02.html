03 / THEORY

Refraction, prism geometry & minimum deviation

When light passes from one transparent medium into another, its speed changes and the direction of propagation generally changes. This bending is refraction. For two media, Snell's law relates the angles to their refractive indices.

sin i / sin r = n₂ / n₁

Here i is the angle of incidence, r is the angle of refraction, and n₁ and n₂ are the refractive indices of the first and second media. The refractive index is also related to the speed of light through the medium.

v₂ / v₁ = n₁ / n₂

For a prism of angle A, a ray enters the first face, refracts inside the prism and emerges from the second face. The angle between the original direction of the incident ray and the emergent ray is the angle of deviation, δ.

As the prism is rotated, the deviation first decreases, reaches a minimum, and then increases. At the minimum-deviation position the ray travels through the prism symmetrically: the angle of incidence equals the angle of emergence. If the minimum deviation is δm, the refractive index of the prism is

μ = sin[(A + δₘ)/2] / sin(A/2)

Thus the experiment has two linked measurements. First, determine the prism angle A from the reflected images of the two prism faces. Then determine the minimum deviation δₘ by comparing the refracted image with the direct image. These two quantities are substituted into the equation above.

PHYSICAL IDEA

At minimum deviation, the optical path through the prism is symmetric. This is why the special position is so useful: instead of needing the internal refraction angles directly, the measurable prism angle and minimum deviation are sufficient to determine μ.

06 / PROCEDURE

Experimental sequence

Part A — angle of the prism

  1. Calibrate the spectrometer by focusing the telescope on a distant object.
  2. Determine the least count of the spectrometer.
  3. Place the prism on the prism table with its refracting edge at the centre and towards the collimator.
  4. Observe the light reflected from the first polished face through the telescope, focus the slit image on the cross-wire and note the telescope reading.
  5. Rotate the telescope to observe the reflected image from the second polished face and note the second reading.
  6. The difference between the two readings gives twice the prism angle, 2A. Use the magnitude of the difference; if the circular-scale difference is greater than 180°, apply the instrument correction 360° − difference. Then take half to obtain A.

Part B — angle of minimum deviation

  1. Rotate the prism table so that light from the collimator enters one face and emerges through the other.
  2. Turn the telescope to observe the refracted slit image and bring it onto the cross-wire.
  3. Continue rotating the prism table in the same direction. The image approaches a position where it becomes momentarily stationary and then begins to move back.
  4. Fix the prism table at this stationary position and note the two vernier readings.
  5. Remove the prism and observe the direct slit image through the telescope. Note the two direct-ray readings.
  6. The magnitude of the difference between the minimum-deviation position and the direct position gives δₘ. If the circular-scale difference exceeds 180°, use 360° − difference to obtain the corresponding smaller angular separation.
  7. Substitute A and δₘ into the prism formula to calculate μ.

09 / VIVA PRACTICE

Test your understanding

Think in terms of the change in the speed of light between media. The direction changes because the wavefront changes its speed at the boundary; Snell's law describes the resulting relationship between the angles.
The geometry of incidence and emergence changes continuously. There is one special orientation where the path is symmetric and the deviation reaches its smallest value. Moving away from that orientation increases the deviation again.
The refracted image becomes momentarily stationary as the prism table is rotated in the chosen direction. After that point the image begins to move back, so the stationary position is used as the minimum-deviation position.
The direct image provides the reference direction of the incident beam. Comparing it with the refracted image tells us how much the ray has actually been deviated by the prism.
At minimum deviation the path is symmetric, so the angle of incidence equals the angle of emergence. That symmetry lets the refractive index be written directly in terms of the measurable prism angle A and minimum deviation δₘ.
Their minimum deviations would generally differ because the refractive indices differ. A larger refractive index produces a larger deviation for the same prism geometry at the minimum-deviation condition.

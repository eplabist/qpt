03 / THEORY

From photons to stopping potential

When radiation falls on a photosensitive metal surface, electrons may be emitted. This phenomenon is called photoelectric emission. The experiment shows that the emission depends strongly on the frequency of the incident radiation, not simply on its intensity.

THE KEY EXPERIMENTAL FACTS
  1. i
    For a given photosensitive material, there is a threshold (critical) frequency below which photoelectrons are not emitted.
  2. ii
    When the frequency is above threshold, emission occurs essentially without an appreciable time delay.
  3. iii
    At fixed frequency above threshold, increasing the intensity mainly increases the number of emitted electrons and hence the photocurrent.

Einstein explained these observations by treating light as a stream of photons. Each photon has energy proportional to its frequency:

E = hν = hc/λ

The electron is bound to the material surface by an energy called the work function, ϕ. If the photon energy is less than the work function, the electron cannot escape. If the photon energy exceeds the work function, the excess becomes the maximum kinetic energy of the photoelectron.

Kmax = hν − ϕ

To measure that maximum kinetic energy, a reverse or retarding potential is applied. As the reverse voltage is increased, fewer emitted electrons reach the collector. At the stopping potential Vs, even the most energetic photoelectrons are just prevented from reaching the collector.

eVs = hν − ϕ

Therefore, the stopping potential should vary linearly with frequency:

Vs = (h/e)ν − ϕ/e

A plot of Vs against ν should therefore be approximately a straight line. Its slope is h/e, so h = e × slope. The y-intercept is −ϕ/e, so the work function is obtained from the magnitude of the intercept: ϕ = e × |y-intercept|.

ν0 = ϕ/h    (at Vs = 0)
INTUITIVE CHECK

Think of frequency as the energy-per-photon control and intensity as the photon-count control. Increasing intensity at a frequency below threshold gives more photons, but each photon is still individually too weak to eject an electron. Increasing frequency above threshold gives each photon more energy, which increases the maximum kinetic energy and hence the stopping potential.

05 / PROCEDURE

Experimental sequence

  1. Set the de-accelerating voltage to 0 V and insert the first colour filter. The manual gives a red filter of approximately 635 nm as an example.
  2. Set the light-intensity control to its maximum value.
  3. Increase the reverse (de-accelerating) voltage gradually until the ammeter/current reading becomes zero.
  4. Record this reverse voltage as the stopping potential Vs for that filter.
  5. Repeat the same procedure for the remaining colour filters.
  6. Enter the measured frequency ν and stopping potential Vs for each colour filter in the observation table.
  7. Plot Vs against ν. Use the slope to determine Planck's constant and the intercept to determine the work function.

07 / CALCULATIONS

Extracting h and the work function

Vs = (h/e)ν − ϕ/e = mν + c

The graph is fitted using the ordinary least-squares linear regression method. For the measured pairs (ν, Vs), the calculator determines the best-fit line Vs = mν + c.

LINEAR REGRESSION ALGORITHM
  1. Let x = ν and y = Vs for each complete observation.
  2. Calculate the mean values x̄ and ȳ.
  3. Calculate the slope: m = Σ(x − x̄)(y − ȳ) / Σ(x − x̄)².
  4. Calculate the y-intercept: c = ȳ − mx̄.
  5. The fitted line is Vs = mν + c. The coefficient of determination R² is also calculated to indicate the quality of the fit.
Planck's constant:   h = e × slope = e × m
Work function:   ϕ = e × |y-intercept| = e × |c|
Threshold frequency:   ν0 = −c/m = ϕ/h

Here e is the electronic charge. The digital calculator performs a least-squares fit to the entered observations; the graph and numerical outputs are intended to reproduce the calculation described in the manual.

09 / VIVA PRACTICE

Test your understanding

Intensity tells you how many photons arrive, but each photon still carries too little energy to overcome the work function. More low-energy photons do not make an individual photon energetic enough to eject an electron.
The higher-frequency photons carry more energy per photon. If both frequencies are above threshold, the maximum kinetic energy of the emitted electrons and therefore the stopping potential should be larger for the higher frequency.
At the stopping potential, the reverse electric field is just strong enough to prevent even the most energetic photoelectrons from reaching the collector. Thus eVₛ corresponds to the maximum kinetic energy.
Increasing frequency increases the energy of each photon, so the maximum electron energy and stopping potential rise. Intensity controls the rate at which photons arrive and therefore mainly affects how many electrons are emitted and the photocurrent.
Einstein's equation is eVₛ = hν − ϕ. Rearranging gives Vₛ = (h/e)ν − ϕ/e, which has exactly the form y = mx + c.
Check the entered frequencies, voltage calibration, zero offset, contact-potential effects, electrical connections and whether the stopping-potential readings were taken consistently. A slope is meaningful only when the systematic effects are under control.

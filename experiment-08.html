03 / THEORY

How does a p-n junction diode respond to voltage?

A p-n junction diode is a two-terminal semiconductor device formed by joining p-type and n-type regions. At equilibrium, a depletion region and built-in potential form around the junction. Applying an external voltage changes the junction barrier and therefore the current through the device.

In forward bias, the p-side is connected to the positive terminal and the n-side to the negative terminal. The applied voltage reduces the effective barrier and narrows the depletion region, allowing majority carriers to cross the junction. The forward current rises rapidly after the characteristic knee (cut-in) voltage.

I = IS [ exp(VD / ηVT) − 1 ]

Here IS is the reverse saturation current, η is the ideality factor, and VT = kT/q is the thermal voltage. The ideal diode equation describes the exponential trend; a real diode also includes series resistance and other non-ideal effects.

In reverse bias, the n-side is connected to the positive terminal and the p-side to the negative terminal. The depletion region widens and the current is small over the reverse-saturation region. At sufficiently high reverse voltage, breakdown can occur and the reverse current rises sharply. A general-purpose diode should not be driven into breakdown unless the device is specifically designed for it.

For a silicon diode, a commonly quoted room-temperature knee voltage is about 0.7 V; for a germanium diode it is about 0.3 V. These are typical values rather than universal constants and depend on device and measurement conditions.

rd = ΔV / ΔI

The dynamic (small-signal) resistance at a point on the characteristic is obtained from the local slope. A simple dc resistance at a chosen operating point is R = V/I.

07 / PROCEDURE

Experimental sequence

  1. Switch off the supply and make the forward-bias circuit with the p-side connected to the positive terminal and the n-side to the negative terminal. Use a suitable series resistance to limit current.
  2. Connect the voltmeter across the diode and the milliammeter in series with the diode. Check the polarity and meter ranges before switching on the supply.
  3. Increase the applied voltage gradually. For each setting, record the voltage across the diode V and the forward current I in the observation table. Use smaller voltage steps near the knee region.
  4. Continue the forward-bias measurements only within the safe current rating of the diode and the selected meter range.
  5. Switch off the supply. Reverse the diode connections to obtain reverse bias, so that the n-side is positive with respect to the p-side. Change to the appropriate low-current meter range.
  6. Increase the reverse-bias voltage gradually and record the reverse current. Do not approach breakdown unless the diode is specifically rated for the experiment.
  7. Plot I versus V separately for forward and reverse bias. Identify the knee region from the forward characteristic and obtain resistance from the appropriate slope or operating point.

09 / CALCULATIONS

From measured V–I data to diode parameters

Rdc = V / I
rd ≈ ΔV / ΔI
Vk = knee / cut-in voltage read from the forward characteristic

For the forward characteristic, the dc resistance at a measured point is Rdc = V/I. The dynamic resistance is estimated from the slope between two nearby forward-bias points. For reverse bias, the magnitude |V|/|I| gives the dc resistance at that reading. The knee voltage is read from the plotted forward characteristic rather than treated as a universal constant.

11 / VIVA PRACTICE

Test your understanding

It is a two-terminal device whose junction structure produces strongly asymmetric current–voltage behavior: current is much easier in forward bias than in reverse bias over the normal operating range.
The applied polarity opposes the built-in junction field, reducing the effective barrier and depletion width so that majority carriers can cross the junction more readily.
In reverse bias, the barrier increases and majority-carrier injection is suppressed. The measured current is mainly associated with minority carriers and generation processes, so it is much smaller before breakdown.
It marks the region of the forward characteristic where the current begins increasing rapidly. It is a practical characteristic of the measured diode, not an exact step-function threshold.
Once the diode enters strong forward conduction, a small increase in voltage can produce a large current increase. The resistor limits current and protects the diode and measuring instruments.
Dc resistance at an operating point is V/I. Dynamic resistance describes the local slope of the characteristic and is approximated by ΔV/ΔI over a small interval.
The reverse current rises sharply when the applied reverse voltage reaches the device’s breakdown region. The mechanism may involve Zener or avalanche processes depending on the junction and doping conditions.
